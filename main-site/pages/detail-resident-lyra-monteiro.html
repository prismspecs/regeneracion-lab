Dr. Lyra D. Monteiro

Scholar-in-Residence 2024-2026

Dr. Lyra D. Monteiro (she/zie) is a scholar, artist, and organizer whose work focuses on the uses of the past in public culture. Her interdisciplinary training includes a BA in Classics and Anthropology (NYU); MAs in Latin and in Classical Art & Archaeology (University of Michigan, Ann Arbor); and an MA in Public Humanities from the American Studies department at Brown University, where she also earned her PhD from the Joukowsky Institute for Archaeology and the Ancient World. Dr. Monteiro directs The Museum On Site, a public arts/public humanities project, and is Co-Convener of Finding Ceremony, a descendant-led process for the return of ancestral remains, with West Philadelphia writer and organizer aAliy Muhammad. She is writing two books: UnCollect Our Ancestors, about Finding Ceremony’s work for the repatriation of ancestors held by museums; and Liberation Archaeology, which offers people of color in the US a liberatory way to engage with our past(s).

About the Work

Dr. Monteiro's focus as a Scholar-in-Residence at the Regeneracion Lab is on the book she is writing about the ongoing work for the return of stolen ancestral remains held at the Penn Museum. This book, titled, UnCollect Our Ancestors: Finding Ceremony and the Descendant Community Struggle for the Return of the Penn Museum’s Morton Cranial Collection (under advance contract at Penn Press). The primary audience for this book is descendants of the 1,300 people whose skulls are currently part of the Penn Museum’s Morton Cranial Collection, assembled by Dr. Samuel George Morton, one of the founders of American racial science. This audience includes both those who Finding Ceremony is able to identify, through archival and genealogical research, as direct lineal descendants of specific individuals in the Morton Collection; as well as those who identify as part of a broader descendant community (in the sense first articulated by Dr. Michael Blakey to describe community response to the desecration of NYC’s African Burial Ground). Last year, she drafted the history section of the book; recruited and began working with five Community Editors, each of whom represents a different community impacted by Morton’s theft of ancestors; as well as organized and fundraised for three descendant community members and researchers with Finding Ceremony to attend and share their work at the American Anthropological Association's Annual Meeting, in a roundtable that is now being prepared for publication in the journal Museum Anthropology. This year she plans to research and write the section of the book that focuses on other models for the return of ancestral remains that are held by museums and other colonial institutions.
